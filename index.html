How Duval Local Harvest works

Food grown local, cooked and served, and what's left over sorted by what it is — surplus fed to people first, and only true scraps composted into community gardens at the food bank sites.

It's a closed loop, and the last step feeds the first: whatever can't safely feed a person becomes compost for the community gardens at the food bank sites, growing the next round of food for the same people.

The recovery strategy

Sort surplus by what it is

Not all leftover food is the same. Sorting it by type decides where it can safely go.

Raw & packaged

Untouched produce and sealed items. Safe, high-volume — the easy lane, and it feeds people.

Feeds people

Prepared food

Strict temperature rules apply. Some is recoverable with the right handling; much of it becomes compost.

Handle with care

What's left

Trimmings and anything that can't safely feed a person. It still has a use.

Compost, never landfill

Feed people first, compost the rest. That's the order the EPA's Wasted Food Scale prioritizes. Food donors are protected by the federal Bill Emerson Good Samaritan Food Donation Act and Florida Statute 768.136.

Who's on the network

These are the farms on the Duval Local Harvest network right now — growers who have joined to sell to Jacksonville kitchens through DLH. Most are in Duval County. A farm gets a pin on the map once we have its location; every farm is in the list under the map.

The pins match our logo. A barn is a farm, coloured by what it grows. The plate is a restaurant on the network, with what it's looking for. The box is a food bank and the truck is food rescue: what a farm posts and no kitchen claims is meant to go to them, not to waste.

And there is a hole in this map we want you to see. Clay County alone has more than 400 farms — cattle, hay, timber, bees, hydroponics, aquaponics, vegetables, fruit and citrus, according to UF/IFAS Extension — and almost none are listed anywhere a chef would think to look. That gap is the reason Duval Local Harvest exists. If you farm in Duval, Nassau, Baker, Clay or St. Johns and you're not on this map, apply to join.

The growing calendar

What's actually in season here

Northeast Florida runs on an inverted calendar — our hardest month is August, not January, and greens are a winter crop. Six two-month blocks, because a season here turns over faster than a quarter.

A calendar, not a guarantee. This is what the region grows and lands in a typical year — weather, acreage and a given farm's planting decide what's actually available in any given week. Seafood entries reflect regional harvest windows, not crops. Always confirm with the grower before you print it on a menu.

Two sources. The regional backbone is adapted with thanks from “What’s in Season” by Edible Northeast Florida, compiled from the Florida Department of Agriculture, FWC and the South Atlantic Fishery Management Council; we list Datil in place of their general “hot peppers.” The tropical greens, roselle, Tokyo bekana and the full herb list come from Nathan Ballentine of Overalls Farm, from seven years of his own harvest records and thirty years growing in North Florida.
